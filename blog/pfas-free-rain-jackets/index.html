A few years ago, finding a genuinely PFAS-free rain jacket that actually worked was a project. You could spend an evening cross-referencing brand sustainability pages, decoding "PFC-free vs. PFAS-free" marketing language, and still end up unsure. That's changed quickly. New York's and California's apparel PFAS bans took effect on January 1, 2025, and the outdoor industry's largest membrane supplier, W.L. Gore, has begun transitioning the majority of its consumer portfolio away from fluorinated chemistry. The result: there are now real, in-stock, PFAS-free rain jackets at almost every price point, from $50 kids' sets up to $650 alpine shells.

This guide pulls together the brands and product lines that have publicly committed to PFAS-free waterproofing, organized by how you'd actually shop — technical performance, everyday wear, style-forward, kids, and a few specialty options worth knowing about. I'll also walk through what "PFAS-free" actually means on a product page, the small performance tradeoffs to expect, and how to verify a specific item before you buy.

How Rain Jackets Stay Waterproof

To shop intelligently for a PFAS-free rain jacket, it helps to understand how a rain jacket actually works. Most modern waterproof shells have two distinct layers doing two different jobs.

The membrane is the inner waterproof barrier. This is the part that physically keeps water out — historically Gore-Tex (made with expanded PTFE, a PFAS), but increasingly newer membranes like Gore's ePE (expanded polyethylene), Pertex, or proprietary alternatives from individual brands. The membrane is laminated to the inside of the outer fabric, and it's the layer doing the heavy lifting on waterproofness.

The DWR (Durable Water Repellent) is a coating applied to the outside of the fabric. DWR isn't what makes a jacket waterproof — that's the job of the membrane underneath — but it helps the garment breathe and dry more effectively by causing water to bead up and roll off the surface rather than soaking the outer face fabric. When DWR wears off, the outer fabric "wets out" — it gets visibly saturated, the jacket feels clammy, and breathability tanks. The membrane is still working, but the experience of wearing the jacket gets miserable.

Both layers have historically used PFAS chemistry. The membrane could be PTFE-based. The DWR was almost always fluorinated. A truly PFAS-free rain jacket has to address both — which is why brand language matters so much.

What "PFAS-Free" Means on a Product Page

Product page language is not standardized, and that's where most of the confusion lives. A few terms worth knowing.

"PFAS-free" is the strongest claim. It generally means no per- or polyfluoroalkyl substances are intentionally added at any stage — fabric, DWR, membrane. Look for this exact phrasing.

"PFC-free" is an older but functionally equivalent term. PFCs (perfluorinated chemicals) are a subset of PFAS, and in apparel marketing the two are used interchangeably. A "PFC-free" rain jacket from 2022 onward should be PFAS-free in practice.

"Fluorine-free DWR" specifically addresses the outer coating. If a jacket has fluorine-free DWR but a PFAS-based membrane underneath, it's only half PFAS-free. This is a real category — Columbia, for example, has historically sold both fully PFAS-free OutDry Extreme lines and short-chain PFC products simultaneously. Always check both layers.

"Bluesign-approved" or "Oeko-Tex Standard 100 certified" are third-party certifications that restrict PFAS as part of broader chemistry audits. As of January 2025, all Bluesign-approved materials must be free from intentionally added PFAS. These are the certifications to look for when reading clothing labels.

Vague claims to be skeptical of: "natural," "eco-friendly," "clean," "sustainable" — none of these are regulated terms in apparel, and none of them tell you anything specific about PFAS.

Performance Tradeoffs to Know

PFAS-free DWRs work just as well as legacy fluorinated DWRs when new. But they're more susceptible to contamination from dirt, sweat, and abrasion, so they may lose their effectiveness more quickly and require more frequent care and maintenance.

What this means practically:

  • You'll wash and reproof more often. Plan to clean a PFAS-free shell every 10–15 wears in active use, and reproof every 1–2 seasons depending on conditions.
  • Oil and grease resistance is reduced. This isn't usually an issue for rain jackets — it matters more for stain-resistant pants. But if you're someone who eats greasy trail food in your shell, expect more visible spots.
  • The "feel" is similar. Modern PFAS-free shells from Patagonia, Arc'teryx, Mountain Equipment, and others are essentially indistinguishable from their legacy versions in normal wear. Reviewers report comparable waterproofness and breathability.
  • Care products matter. Use a fluorine-free reproofing spray like Nikwax TX.Direct or Granger's Performance Repel Plus. Never use traditional Scotchgard or fluorinated reproofers, which would defeat the entire purpose.

A Quick Note on Reproofing

PFAS-free DWRs wear off faster than the legacy fluorinated versions, so plan on reproofing every 1–2 seasons of regular use. The standard safer options:

  • Nikwax TX.Direct (wash-in or spray-on) — identified by the Washington Department of Ecology as a safer alternative, used by Páramo and recommended by Toxic-Free Future.
  • Granger's Performance Repel Plus — PFAS-free spray-on, widely available.
  • Storm Care Eco-Proof — fluorocarbon-free alternative for European-market jackets.

Avoid traditional Scotchgard products containing PFAS — older formulations from 3M almost certainly contain forever chemicals, which would defeat the purpose of buying a PFAS-free shell.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there really PFAS-free rain jackets that actually work?

Yes — and the gap has closed quickly. Modern PFAS-free DWRs and membranes (Gore-Tex ePE, Atmos, OutDry Extreme, Páramo's Nikwax-treated system) are essentially indistinguishable from their fluorinated predecessors in normal use. Toxic-Free Future, NRDC, and the Washington Department of Ecology have all confirmed that safer alternatives to PFAS are available for raingear. The main tradeoff is that PFAS-free DWRs need more frequent reproofing.

What's the most affordable PFAS-free rain jacket?

For adults, REI Co-op's in-house rain jackets start around $70 and are fully PFAS-free as of Fall 2024. For kids, Reima starts at $50 for a basic raincoat. If you can stretch slightly, Royal Robbins, Columbia OutDry Extreme, and Cotopaxi Cielo all sit in the $130–$200 range.

Is Patagonia really PFAS-free?

Yes, as of Spring 2025. Patagonia has committed to and reports completion of PFAS-free membranes and water-repellent finishes across the line. They were the only outdoor brand to earn a "B" on the 2022 NRDC scorecard — the highest grade in the category — and have followed through on that public commitment.

What about Gore-Tex? Is it PFAS-free now?

Some Gore-Tex products are. Gore introduced its expanded polyethylene (ePE) membrane as a PFAS-free alternative to its traditional PTFE membranes, and is transitioning the majority of its consumer portfolio away from fluorinated chemistry by the end of 2025. Look for jackets specifically labeled "Gore-Tex ePE" — not all Gore-Tex products are ePE yet.

Do PFAS-free rain jackets need more care?

A bit more, yes. The PFAS-free DWR coating is more susceptible to contamination from dirt, sweat, and abrasion, so it may lose effectiveness more quickly. Plan to wash with a specialist cleaner (not household detergent) every 10–15 wears, and reproof every 1–2 seasons with a fluorine-free product. The care label guide covers washing symbols in detail if you're unsure what your jacket requires.

Are kids' PFAS-free raincoats different from adult ones?

The chemistry is the same — what differs is the design (cuff and hood fit, durability for high-contact use) and the additional question of PVC. Cheap, shiny "vinyl" children's raincoats often contain phthalates, hormone-disrupting plasticizers that can leach through skin contact. The best kids' brands (Reima, Fairechild, Polarn O. Pyret) avoid both PFAS and PVC.

Should I throw out my current PFAS-treated rain jacket?

Most experts say no. Experts believe there is actually very little risk to a person who continues to wear their PFAS-treated rain jackets — the larger exposure pathways are drinking water, food packaging, and indoor dust. The pragmatic move is to wear out what you have, then choose PFAS-free for the next one.
