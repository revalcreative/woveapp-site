Sustainable Fabric Blends Explained: What Shoppers Should Know

Fabric blends are everywhere — but not all blends are created equal. Here's how to evaluate whether a blend actually makes a garment better or just cheaper.

By Emily Hemphill · 4 min read · Published August 2026

Most garments are blends of two or more fibers. The question is whether that blend serves you — adding durability, stretch, or performance — or whether it’s primarily a cost-cutting measure that complicates the garment’s end-of-life. Here’s how to tell the difference.

What a Fabric Blend Is

A fabric blend combines two or more fiber types in a single fabric. The blending can happen at the fiber stage (fibers spun together into a single yarn), the yarn stage (different yarns plied together), or the fabric stage (different yarns woven or knit together in patterns).

The most common reason for blending is to combine the desirable properties of different fibers:

  • Cotton + polyester: Cotton’s breathability and feel + polyester’s wrinkle resistance and durability
  • Merino + nylon: Merino’s softness and odor resistance + nylon’s durability at wear points
  • Linen + cotton: Linen’s breathability and texture + cotton’s softness and ease of care
  • Cotton + spandex: Cotton’s natural feel + spandex’s stretch

Why Brands Use Blends

Performance engineering: Blends can hit performance targets that a single fiber can’t. A 90/10 merino/nylon blend is more durable than pure merino without sacrificing much of the softness. A 95/5 cotton/spandex is more comfortable in fitted garments than 100% cotton.

Cost management: Adding cheaper fibers to expensive ones reduces material cost. A 55% linen / 45% polyester blend is cheaper than 100% linen. This isn’t inherently dishonest, but it’s worth understanding when the blend serves you versus when it serves the brand’s margin.

Improved care: All-linen wrinkles aggressively; a cotton-linen blend is somewhat easier to care for. All-silk is fragile; a silk-cotton blend is more forgiving.

Recycled material inclusion: Brands increasingly use blended recycled polyester or recycled nylon alongside natural fibers. This addresses input-side concerns but creates recyclability trade-offs.

When Blends Help and When They Don’t

Blends that serve the wearer:

  • Small synthetic % for stretch (2–5% spandex in otherwise natural-fiber garment)
  • Small nylon % for durability at wear points in a merino garment (5–10%)
  • Natural fiber combinations (linen/cotton, wool/silk) that combine complementary properties
  • Recycled content in synthetics (addresses input; doesn’t change wear performance)

Blends that primarily serve the brand:

  • High synthetic % in what markets as a natural fiber product (a “cotton” garment that’s 45% polyester)
  • Synthetic inclusions that reduce recyclability without adding meaningful performance
  • Blending to hit a lower price point while marketing as “natural” or “sustainable”

How to Evaluate a Blend

Step 1: Identify the dominant fiber. The highest-percentage fiber sets the primary character of the garment. A 70% merino / 30% polyester is a synthetic-blended merino — meaningfully different from a 30% merino / 70% polyester synthetic with some merino in it.

Step 2: Ask what each minor fiber adds. A few percent of spandex adds stretch. Ten percent of nylon adds durability. Thirty percent of polyester probably adds cost control, not performance.

Step 3: Consider end-of-life. Blends of natural and synthetic fibers typically can’t be mechanically recycled through current systems. If circularity matters to you, all-natural or all-synthetic is more recyclable than a mix.

Step 4: Check certification. A GOTS-certified cotton/spandex blend still meets GOTS standards. An uncertified “organic cotton / recycled polyester” blend may or may not.

FAQ

Is a recycled polyester + cotton blend “sustainable”?
Partially. The recycled polyester addresses input sourcing; the cotton can be organic. But the blend is harder to recycle at end-of-life than either fiber alone, and the polyester still sheds microplastics during washing. Sustainability is a spectrum — this is better than conventional polyester alone, but not fully circular.

Are blends always lower quality than single-fiber fabrics?
No. Some blends — merino/nylon, for example — outperform either fiber alone for specific uses. Quality depends on fiber grade, blend ratios, and construction, not on whether it’s a blend.

What percentage of synthetic fiber in a blend causes microplastic shedding?
Any synthetic content sheds its proportion of microplastics. A 10% polyester content sheds about 10% as much as a 100% polyester garment with the same construction and weight.

What’s the most “sustainable” blend?
Depends on what you prioritize. For low chemical concern and minimal microplastic shedding: GOTS-certified cotton/linen or merino/silk. For durability and performance: merino/nylon with bluesign® certification. For price-accessible certified option: GOTS organic cotton/spandex.
