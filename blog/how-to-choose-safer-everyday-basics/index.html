How to Choose Safer Everyday Basics

Everyday clothing basics are worn close to the skin, washed often, and replaced regularly. Here's how to choose ones that hold up well and raise fewer concerns.

By Emily Hemphill · 5 min read · Published August 2026

T-shirts, underwear, socks, and base layers are the clothing we’re in contact with most — against skin, all day, washed frequently. They’re also the easiest category to upgrade if you’re trying to reduce chemical exposure, because the bar for “safer” here is largely about choosing better fibers and avoiding unnecessary treatments.



Prioritize What’s Closest to Skin


Underwear, bras, base layers

What Makes a Basic “Safer”

The term “safer” in clothing refers to two things in this context:

Fewer chemical concerns: Clothing worn close to skin over long periods provides more opportunity for chemical exposure than outerwear. Basics made from certified natural fibers — organic cotton, linen, wool — or certified synthetics with PFAS-free claims minimize this.

Less microplastic shedding: Basics washed frequently shed more total microplastics over time than items washed less often. Natural fibers shed natural fibers, not plastic. Synthetics shed microplastics with every wash.

Neither of these concerns makes a given garment dangerous at normal exposure levels. But if you’re building a wardrobe with intention, basics are the highest-leverage place to start.

Fabrics to Prioritize

Organic cotton: GOTS-certified organic cotton is grown without most synthetic pesticides and processed without certain chemical treatments. It’s a strong default choice for t-shirts, underwear, and other basics. Look for GOTS or OEKO-TEX® certification rather than just the word “organic.”

GOTS-certified cotton: GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard) covers both fiber content and processing — it’s more comprehensive than “organic cotton” alone, which only describes the farming stage.

Linen: Made from flax, linen is naturally moisture-wicking, breathable, and durable. It doesn’t require pesticide-heavy cultivation and doesn’t need chemical treatments for its performance properties. A strong option for warm-weather basics.

Merino wool: One of the highest-performing natural fibers for base layers. It regulates temperature, resists odor naturally (so you wash it less), and doesn’t shed microplastics. MULESING-FREE labeling is something to look for if animal welfare is a concern.

Bamboo viscose: Often marketed for softness and moisture-wicking. The fiber itself is benign, but the viscose processing uses significant chemical inputs — look for OEKO-TEX® certification on the finished garment to confirm the final product is clean.

Red Flags to Watch For

“Moisture-wicking” on a polyester t-shirt without certification: Moisture-wicking polyester isn’t inherently PFAS-treated, but it sheds microplastics. If you’re choosing between polyester and cotton for an everyday t-shirt, cotton will shed natural fibers rather than plastic.

“Stain-resistant” on underwear or base layers: This claim in direct skin-contact garments is worth investigating. Stain resistance treatments that include PFAS in a base layer worn all day are a higher concern than the same treatment on a jacket you wear occasionally.

“Wrinkle-free” or “no-iron” cotton dress shirts: This finish is typically achieved with formaldehyde-based resins — a separate but real concern for daily-wear items.

No certifications and no material disclosures: Low-cost fast-fashion basics often have no certification and use commodity cotton and synthetics with no chemical management. This doesn’t make them automatically harmful, but it does mean you have less information.

How to Build a Better Closet

The goal isn’t to replace everything at once — it’s to make better choices at natural replacement points.

Start with what’s next to your skin. Underwear, socks, and base layers are high-priority upgrades because they’re in direct skin contact and washed frequently.

Work from most-worn to least. A t-shirt you wear three times a week is a higher-priority swap than a dress shirt you wear once a month.

Use certification as your shortcut. You don’t need to memorize every fabric’s chemistry. GOTS or OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 on the tag or product page is a reliable quick filter.

Don’t let perfect be the enemy of better. An organic cotton t-shirt without GOTS certification is still better than uncertified conventional cotton. Progress beats perfection.

FAQ

Is organic cotton always safer than conventional?
Organic cotton is grown without most synthetic pesticides, which is a meaningful difference for the farming stage. For the finished garment, GOTS certification is stronger because it covers the whole production chain including dyes and finishing.

What about Modal and Tencel?
Both are cellulose-based fibers made from wood pulp (beech and eucalyptus, respectively). They’re generally considered lower-concern than synthetics for microplastic shedding, though they shed some fibers. Look for OEKO-TEX® certification on the finished garment.

Is it worth spending more on certified basics?
Cost per wear matters more than sticker price. A GOTS-certified organic cotton t-shirt that lasts five years and costs $40 has a lower cost-per-wear than a $10 fast-fashion version that fades in a year and contributes to more textile waste.

How do I know if a brand’s certification is real?
GOTS and OEKO-TEX® both have public certification databases you can search by brand or facility name. If a brand claims certification, you can verify it.
