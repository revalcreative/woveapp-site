Best Fabrics for Sensitive Skin

If your skin reacts to certain clothing, the fiber content and fabric finish are usually where to look first. Here's what to avoid and what tends to work better.

By Emily Hemphill · 5 min read · Published July 2026

If certain clothing makes your skin itch, flush, or break out, you’re not imagining it. The fiber content, fabric finish, and dye chemistry of clothing can all trigger reactions in people with sensitive or reactive skin. Here’s how to shop with that in mind.

What to Avoid If Your Skin Reacts Easily

Before identifying what to look for, it helps to understand what tends to cause reactions.

Rough or high-micron wool: Not all wool is scratchy — that’s a micron count issue. Wool fibers above ~20 microns can cause mechanical irritation. Fine merino (15–18.5 microns) sits below the threshold for most people and feels soft against skin.

Disperse dyes in synthetic fabrics: Polyester and nylon require a specific dye class called disperse dyes, which bond to synthetic fibers at high temperatures. Disperse dyes are one of the more common sources of contact dermatitis from clothing, particularly from tight-fitting synthetic garments.

Formaldehyde-based wrinkle-free finishes: “Wrinkle-free,” “no-iron,” and “easy care” cotton finishes are often achieved with formaldehyde-based resins. These can cause skin reactions in sensitive individuals, particularly with new garments before washing.

Fabric softeners and residues: Fabric softener coats fibers with compounds that some people react to. Washing clothing before wearing and using fragrance-free, dye-free detergents reduces this risk.

Flame retardants: Certain children’s sleepwear and some activewear uses chemical flame retardants. These can cause skin reactions in sensitive individuals. OEKO-TEX® certification restricts many of these.

Tight synthetics + prolonged wear: The heat and friction of tight synthetic fabric against skin over long periods can cause mechanical irritation even without a chemical trigger.

Soft Natural Fabrics to Consider

Fine Merino Wool

At 15–18.5 microns, fine merino is soft enough for most sensitive skin — including many people who think they’re allergic to wool. (True wool allergy is rare; most “wool reactions” are mechanical irritation from coarser fibers.) Merino is also naturally hypoallergenic in the sense that it doesn’t require chemical anti-itch treatments. Look for OEKO-TEX® certification on the finished garment.

Organic Cotton (GOTS-Certified)

GOTS-certified organic cotton is grown without most synthetic pesticides and processed without certain chemical treatments, reducing the chemical load on the finished fabric. For sensitive skin, GOTS is a stronger signal than “organic cotton” alone, which only covers farming.

Bamboo Viscose (OEKO-TEX® Certified)

Bamboo viscose is genuinely soft and smooth against skin — the fiber structure is round and uniform, which creates a silky texture. It’s a good option for sensitive skin when OEKO-TEX® certified, which confirms the finished garment meets a restricted substances standard. (Note: the viscose manufacturing process uses chemical inputs — certification covers whether those chemicals are present in the finished product.)

Silk

Silk is a natural protein fiber with a very smooth surface — there are no rough fiber ends to cause mechanical irritation. It’s hypoallergenic by structure. Silk has very low chemical treatment requirements. The main considerations are cost and care (typically hand-wash or dry clean).

Linen

Linen can feel slightly stiff when new but softens significantly with washing. For many people with sensitive skin, the breathability and natural fiber content makes linen more comfortable than cotton over the course of a hot day. Look for OEKO-TEX® certification.

Why Finish and Construction Matter

Even within the same fiber type, the finish and construction change how a fabric interacts with skin.

Finish: A GOTS-certified organic cotton t-shirt and a “wrinkle-free” organic cotton dress shirt are very different products. The wrinkle-free finish adds formaldehyde-based resins; the plain tee doesn’t. The fiber content is the same; the chemical load is not.

Surface texture: A smooth, flat-knit fabric causes less mechanical friction against skin than a textured, embossed, or rough-surfaced one. For sensitive areas (underarms, inner thighs, around bra lines), smooth surface texture matters.

Seams: Flat seams and seamless construction cause less chafing than raised seams. Look for “flatlock” seams in activewear for sensitive or eczema-prone skin.

Elastics and trims: The elastic in waistbands and bra straps is often synthetic even when the main fabric is natural. Some elastics cause reactions — particularly those with latex or certain colorants. If elastic bands are a trigger, look for “latex-free” products.

How to Shop with Sensitivity in Mind

  1. Start with certification. GOTS for cotton, OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 for any fiber type. These are tested standards, not marketing claims.
  2. Wash before first wear. New garments can carry dye or finish residue. Washing before first wear reduces this.
  3. Use fragrance-free, dye-free detergent. The laundry chemistry matters as much as the fabric chemistry.
  4. Test new fabrics gradually. Wear a new fabric for a few hours before committing — reactions often appear within a few wears.
  5. Check the full label. “100% cotton” may still have undisclosed finishes. Certification tells you more than fiber content alone.

FAQ

Am I allergic to wool or just sensitive to coarse fibers?
Most likely the latter. True wool protein allergy is uncommon. Coarse wool fibers cause mechanical irritation to most people — the solution is finer micron wool (merino, cashmere), not avoiding wool altogether.

Does washing clothes before wearing help?
Yes. Pre-washing removes surface dye and finish residue that can cause initial reactions. Use fragrance-free detergent and skip fabric softener.

What’s the safest fabric for a baby or newborn?
GOTS-certified organic cotton is the standard recommendation. It has low chemical inputs, is soft against delicate skin, and is well-tested. Merino wool in fine gauges is also a popular choice.

Is OEKO-TEX® the same as hypoallergenic?
OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 tests for a list of restricted substances — a real, meaningful standard. “Hypoallergenic” is an unregulated marketing claim. OEKO-TEX® is more reliable.
