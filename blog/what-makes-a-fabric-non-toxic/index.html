What Makes a Fabric Non-Toxic?

"Non-toxic" in clothing is more complex than the label suggests. Here's what to actually look for — beyond fiber content — when evaluating a garment.

By Emily Hemphill · 5 min read · Published August 2026

“Non-toxic” is a term that appears on clothing labels and brand websites with increasing frequency — but it’s not a regulated claim, and it can mean very different things depending on who’s using it. Here’s what actually goes into making a fabric lower in chemical concern, and how to evaluate what you’re buying.

Fiber Content Is Only One Part

Most conversations about “non-toxic clothing” start and stop at fiber content — organic cotton versus conventional, natural versus synthetic. Fiber origin matters, but it’s one layer of a more complete picture.

The full picture includes:

Fiber origin: Organic cotton reduces pesticide load at the farming stage. Natural fibers don’t require petroleum extraction. These matter.

Processing chemistry: Once fibers are harvested or produced, they’re spun, woven or knit, bleached, and dyed. Each step involves chemicals. Conventional cotton is routinely treated with chlorine bleach and dyed with azo dyes, some of which break down into carcinogenic amines. Synthetic fibers require disperse dyes applied at high temperatures.

Applied finishes: After the fabric is formed, performance finishes are applied — water repellency (often PFAS), stain resistance, wrinkle-free treatment (formaldehyde resins), antimicrobial coatings (silver ions, triclosan, synthetic biocides), and more. These are applied after the fiber and weave are established and aren’t reflected in the fiber content label.

A GOTS-certified organic cotton t-shirt addresses all three layers. An “organic cotton” t-shirt (without GOTS) only certifies the fiber origin. A “natural fiber” garment without any certification could have significant chemical load from processing and finishing.

Dyes, Finishes, and Treatments Matter Too

Dyes

Azo dyes: Used widely in cotton and other natural fiber dyeing. Some azo dyes are benign; certain types can break down into carcinogenic amines. The EU restricts specific azo dyes in consumer textiles; OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 tests for restricted azo dyes.

Disperse dyes: Used in synthetic fiber dyeing. Associated with contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals. OEKO-TEX® tests for these too.

Reactive dyes: Common in cotton, generally lower concern than some azo formulations. Require significant water use.

Vat dyes and natural dyes: Generally lower concern. Natural dyes (from plants, minerals) are increasingly used by smaller brands; some require mordants (metal salts) that have their own chemistry.

Chemical Finishes

PFAS: Applied as water repellent, stain resistant, or oil repellent coatings. Not listed on fiber content labels. Can migrate off fabric during wear and washing. Linked to health concerns at certain exposure levels.

Formaldehyde-based resins: Used for wrinkle-free and easy-care finishes. Formaldehyde is a known carcinogen at higher exposure levels; residue in finished garments is typically low but varies. OEKO-TEX® and GOTS restrict formaldehyde levels.

Antimicrobial treatments: Silver-ion, triclosan, or synthetic biocide treatments for “odor resistance.” Some of these substances have environmental persistence concerns. Natural odor resistance (from merino’s fiber structure, for example) doesn’t require these.

Flame retardants: Some garment categories require flame resistance. Chemical flame retardants — common in children’s sleepwear under certain standards — can include compounds with health and environmental concerns.

Certifications and Claims to Know

GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard): The most comprehensive standard for natural fiber garments. Covers organic fiber sourcing, all processing steps, restricted substances in dyes and finishes, and worker welfare. A GOTS label is the strongest single signal for a “non-toxic” natural fiber garment.

OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100: Tests the finished product for a list of harmful substances regardless of fiber origin. A synthetic polyester garment can carry OEKO-TEX® S100 if it passes the substance tests. Doesn’t address fiber sourcing but confirms the finished garment is within tested limits.

bluesign®: A process-based standard applied to fabric mills, not final products. Restricts PFAS, certain dyes, and other chemicals in the manufacturing process. Common in performance and outdoor fabrics.

Cradle to Cradle Certified™: Broader sustainability standard that includes material health assessments. Less common in apparel but meaningful when present.

“Non-toxic,” “clean,” “natural”: These are unregulated marketing terms. They can mean anything or nothing. Certification is what gives these claims substance.

Questions to Ask Before Buying

Even with this framework, a few specific questions cut through quickly:

  • Does this carry GOTS certification? (For natural fiber garments)
  • Does this carry OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100? (For any garment type)
  • Is there any stain-resistant or water-resistant treatment? If so, is it PFAS-free?
  • Is the fabric labeled “wrinkle-free” or “easy care”? (Signals formaldehyde resin risk)
  • What dye process was used? (Most brands won’t have this readily available — certification is the shortcut)

FAQ

Is “organic” fabric the same as “non-toxic”?
Not fully. Organic certification (like USDA Organic for cotton) covers the farming stage. It doesn’t address processing chemistry, dyes, or applied finishes. GOTS covers the full chain; “organic” alone doesn’t.

Can a synthetic fabric be non-toxic?
In the sense of chemical finishes and dyes, yes — OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 certifies synthetic fabrics against a restricted substances list. Microplastic shedding is a separate concern that certification doesn’t address.

Is non-toxic clothing necessary for adults?
“Non-toxic” is a spectrum, not a binary. For everyday wear, the incremental chemical exposure from uncertified clothing is typically low. For items worn close to skin daily, especially for children or during pregnancy, choosing certified options is a reasonable precaution.

Does washing new clothes remove chemical residue?
Washing before first wear does reduce surface residue — dye excess, finish coating. It doesn’t remove treatments chemically bonded to the fabric.
