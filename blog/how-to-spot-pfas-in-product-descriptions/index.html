PFAS & Fabric Safety

How to Spot PFAS in Product Descriptions Before You Buy

Learn the words, materials, and marketing claims that signal PFAS in clothing — and how to read product pages more critically before adding to cart.

By Emily Hemphill · 5 min read · Published July 2026

Product pages are written to sell — not to disclose every chemical treatment applied to a garment. Learning to read between the lines can save you from unknowingly bringing PFAS into your wardrobe.

Common Words That Can Signal PFAS

PFAS are used in clothing primarily as performance-enhancing coatings. The language brands use to describe those benefits is your first clue. Watch for:

Water and moisture-related terms:
- Durable water repellent (DWR)
- Water-repellent finish
- Waterproof coating
- Moisture-resistant

Stain and soil-related terms:
- Stain-resistant
- Soil-release finish
- Oil-repellent
- Scotchgard™ (3M’s trademarked fluorochemical finish)
- Teflon™ EcoElite (DuPont’s finish — note that some versions are now fluorine-free, but confirm)

Generic performance claims:
- Easy care
- “Stays cleaner longer”
- Anti-soiling

None of these phrases confirms that PFAS are present — stain resistance can be achieved with other chemistries. But these terms warrant a closer look before purchasing.

Materials and Finishes to Watch For

Certain fabric types are more frequently treated with PFAS than others. When you see these materials in a product description, treat them as a prompt to investigate further:

Outerwear shell fabrics:
- Gore-Tex® (Gore has committed to PFC-free products; check the specific product’s compliance status)
- eVent® fabric
- Any fabric described as having a “membrane”

Performance fabrics:
- “Quick-dry” nylon or polyester
- “Performance” or “technical” stretch fabrics
- “Moisture-wicking” fabrics treated for odor and stain resistance

Everyday fabrics with finishes:
- Chinos, khakis, and trousers described as “wrinkle-free” or “no-iron”
- Dress shirts marketed as “stain-resistant”

What Brands May Leave Out

Product pages are not required to disclose chemical treatments. This means there’s often a significant information gap between what’s on the page and what’s on (or in) the fabric.

Specifically, you may not see:
- Whether a DWR coating is fluorocarbon-based or fluorocarbon-free
- Which generation of PFAS chemistry is used (C8 compounds are more restricted than C6, but both raise concerns)
- Whether a water-resistant fabric is membrane-only (no chemical coating) or membrane plus treatment
- Updated formulation status for older product listings

This gap isn’t necessarily deceptive — brands often don’t list standard processing chemistry the same way they’d list materials. But it does mean the absence of a PFAS disclosure isn’t the same as absence of PFAS.

Questions to Ask Before Purchasing

If a product description leaves you uncertain, these questions get you to a clear answer quickly.

For outerwear and performance gear:
- Is the DWR coating on this product fluorocarbon-free?
- Has this jacket been tested as PFAS-free?
- Does this product carry bluesign® or OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 certification?

For everyday clothing:
- Does this fabric have any stain-resistant or water-repellent chemical treatment applied?
- Is any surface finish on this garment fluorocarbon-based?

Most brands with an honest answer will give you one — brands that have reformulated tend to be proud of it. Customer service teams at companies like Patagonia, Cotopaxi, and smaller sustainable brands are often equipped to answer directly. If a brand can’t or won’t answer, that’s information too.

Safer Alternatives to Look For

Once you know what to avoid, it’s easier to spot what to look for. PFAS-free water repellency exists — the technology has improved significantly.

PFAS-free DWR alternatives:
- Wax-based DWR (beeswax or paraffin)
- Silicone-based coatings
- Bio-based repellents (some derived from corn or castor oil)
- C0 fluorocarbon-free chemistry (some brands have adopted this as a bridge solution)

Certifications that restrict PFAS:
- bluesign®: restricts per- and polyfluorinated compounds
- OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100: tests for a restricted substance list that includes certain PFAS
- PFC-free labeling: some brands use this to signal no fluorocarbon content

Fabric choices that skip the issue entirely:
- Wool and boiled wool (naturally water-resistant without coatings)
- Tightly woven cotton canvas (water-resistant by construction)
- Oilcloth and waxed canvas (traditional, non-PFAS repellency)

FAQ

Does “water-resistant” always mean PFAS?
No — but it’s a prompt to investigate. Water resistance can come from fabric construction, PFAS-based coatings, or PFAS-free alternative coatings. The product page often won’t tell you which, so you may need to dig deeper or ask.

Is Gore-Tex® PFAS-free?
Gore committed to eliminating PFCs (a subset of PFAS) from its products. Many Gore-Tex® products are now marketed as ePE or ePTFE-based without PFAS DWR. Check the specific product’s claims, as older inventory may differ.

What does “PFC-free” mean on a label?
PFCs (perfluorinated compounds) are a category within PFAS. A “PFC-free” claim means no fluorochemical coatings were intentionally used. It’s a stronger signal than “water-resistant” alone.

Can I wash PFAS out of a jacket I already own?
Washing won’t remove PFAS from the fabric itself — and the wash water released contains microfibers and PFAS that enter the water system. The chemicals are bonded to the fabric during manufacturing.

Should I avoid all performance fabrics?
Not necessarily. Many performance fabrics are untreated polyester or nylon — the microplastic shedding risk is separate from PFAS. For PFAS specifically, look for explicit “PFC-free” or “PFAS-free” language, or choose naturally high-performance fibers like merino wool.
