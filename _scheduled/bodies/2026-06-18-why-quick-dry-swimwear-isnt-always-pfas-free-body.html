Quick-dry swimwear is everywhere — and it sounds like a straightforward convenience feature. But "quick-dry" on a swimwear label doesn't always mean what it sounds like, and for some products it signals a chemical treatment that includes PFAS. Here's what the claim actually means and how to tell the difference.

What "Quick-Dry" Actually Means

Quick-dry is a performance description, not a regulated term. It means the garment dries faster than standard clothing after getting wet. What varies — and what the label almost never tells you — is how that drying speed is achieved.

For most synthetic swimwear, quick-dry comes from the fiber itself. Polyester and nylon are hydrophobic: they don't absorb water into the fiber structure the way cotton does. Water beads on the surface and evaporates quickly because there's very little of it trapped in the fabric. No added treatment required.

But quick-dry can also come from a chemical finish applied to the fabric surface after it's knit or woven. These coatings push water off the fabric, reduce absorption, and speed evaporation. Some of these finishes are PFAS-free. Others — especially older formulations and some that are still in use — rely on fluorochemical chemistry, meaning they contain PFAS.

The problem is that a garment tag or product listing says "quick-dry" either way. You can't tell from those two words alone whether you're getting fiber-based performance or a chemical coating.

Why Brands Use Water-Repellent Treatments

If synthetics already dry quickly by nature, why would a brand add a water-repellent treatment at all?

A few reasons:

To achieve stain resistance alongside water repellency. PFAS compounds repel both water and oil simultaneously — which is why they've been so widely used. A swimsuit that also resists sunscreen, sand, and food stains requires a different chemistry than one that just dries fast. Fluorochemical treatments do both in one step.

To enhance performance in natural or blended fabrics. A cotton-blend rash guard or a bamboo-fiber beach layer doesn't dry fast on its own. Adding a chemical finish can extend quick-dry marketing claims to fabrics that wouldn't otherwise qualify.

To differentiate in a crowded market. "Quick-dry + stain-resistant" sounds like more value than "quick-dry" alone. Even when the stain-resistance performance is modest, the claim adds perceived value.

Because it worked really well. Fluorochemical finishes are genuinely effective and durable — they hold up through many washes. That's part of why they've been so widely adopted, and why transitioning away from them has been gradual.

How PFAS Can Show Up in Swimwear

PFAS don't come from the nylon or polyester fiber itself — those polymers don't contain fluorine. PFAS enter swimwear through surface treatments applied after the fabric is formed.

The most common scenarios where PFAS are more likely in swimwear:

Stain-resistant kids' swimwear. This is the highest-concern category. Children's suits marketed to resist sunscreen, sand, and grass stains are the most likely to carry fluorochemical treatments — and children's developing bodies are more sensitive to PFAS exposure.

Rash guards and surf shirts with water-repellent claims. A rash guard marketed as water-repellent (not just quick-drying) may carry a DWR-type coating, historically fluorochemical.

Multi-function swimwear ("quick-dry + stain-resistant + water-repellent"). Each additional performance claim increases the likelihood that a chemical treatment is involved, and that it may use PFAS chemistry.

Natural or blended-fiber swimwear marketed as quick-dry. When an inherently absorbent fiber achieves quick-dry performance, something has been applied to make that happen.

The critical thing to understand: chlorinated pool water and saltwater can accelerate the breakdown and migration of chemical surface treatments. PFAS that are bonded to a fabric surface in a stable environment may release faster when exposed to the oxidizing chemistry of pool chlorine, particularly with heat and prolonged skin contact.

What to Look for Instead

You don't have to give up quick-dry performance to avoid PFAS. Here's what to look for:

Plain certified synthetic. OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 certified nylon or polyester swimwear — without stain-resistant or water-repellent treatment claims beyond the fiber's inherent drying speed — is the most straightforward lower-concern choice. The certification tests the finished garment for a restricted substances list that includes PFAS.

Explicit PFAS-free or PFC-free language. Brands that have reformulated away from fluorochemical treatments will say so on their product pages or sustainability pages. "PFC-free," "PFAS-free," or "fluorocarbon-free" are the phrases to look for.

bluesign® certified fabric. bluesign® restricts per- and polyfluorinated compounds across the manufacturing process. Swimwear or rash guards made from bluesign® certified fabric have been produced under a restricted substances standard.

Recycled nylon (ECONYL®) with certification. Recycled nylon from ocean waste performs identically to virgin nylon and dries just as fast. Paired with OEKO-TEX® or bluesign® certification, it's a strong choice. Note: the recycled sourcing addresses where the fiber came from, not whether a finish was applied afterward — certification closes that gap.

Natural fiber cover-ups without treatment claims. For beach layers and cover-ups, linen, cotton voile, and Tencel® don't need any chemical treatment to function well. Avoid the "water-resistant" or "quick-dry finish" claims on natural-fiber beach items.

How to Shop Smarter with Label Reading

When you're looking at swimwear in-store or online, work through these steps:

Step 1: Check the fiber content. If it's plain nylon or polyester, the quick-dry performance is almost certainly from the fiber, not a coating. Lower concern.

Step 2: Read the claims bullet points. Marketing copy on the product page tells you what the brand is selling. "Quick-dry fabric" (fiber-based) is different from "quick-dry finish" or "quick-dry coating" (chemical-based). Watch for "stain-resistant" — that's the highest PFAS signal in swimwear.

Step 3: Look for certification. OEKO-TEX® STANDARD 100 on the product page or hangtag confirms the finished garment meets a restricted substances standard. This is the most reliable shortcut.

Step 4: Check the brand's materials page. Brands that have committed to PFAS-free production typically have a sustainability or materials page that addresses it directly. If the topic is absent entirely, that's a gap worth following up on.

FAQ

Are all quick-dry swimsuits PFAS-free? No. Quick-dry on a plain synthetic swimsuit (nylon or polyester) typically reflects the fiber's natural water-repelling properties and doesn't involve PFAS. But "quick-dry" on a natural-fiber swimsuit or cover-up, or when combined with stain resistance claims, often involves a chemical treatment that may include PFAS. Look for OEKO-TEX® certification or an explicit PFAS-free claim to confirm.

How can I tell if swimwear has PFAS? You can't tell from the fiber content label alone — PFAS are applied finishes, not fibers. Your signals are: stain-resistance claims, water-repellent claims on natural-fiber products, and "quick-dry finish" language. The confirming step is OEKO-TEX® certification (which tests for restricted substances including PFAS) or a direct PFAS-free claim from the brand.

What are safer alternatives to quick-dry swimwear? OEKO-TEX® certified nylon or polyester swimwear without stain-resistance claims is the most practical option. For cover-ups, linen, cotton voile, and Tencel® dry quickly in sun and heat without any chemical treatment. Recycled nylon with certification is also a strong choice.

Does quick-dry mean the fabric is toxic? Not at all. Most synthetic swimwear is quick-dry because of fiber properties, not because of added treatments — and even treated options aren't inherently dangerous at typical exposure levels. The question is whether you have full information about what's in the garment, and certification is what provides that assurance.
