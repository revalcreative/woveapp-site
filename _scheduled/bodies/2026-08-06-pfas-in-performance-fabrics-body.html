Why Performance Fabrics Are Commonly Treated

Performance fabrics are expected to do things natural fibers weren’t designed to do in their raw state: repel water at the surface while releasing sweat vapor, resist stains during high-output activity, and maintain function wash after wash. For decades, fluorochemical PFAS were the most efficient way to engineer those properties.

PFAS molecules are both hydrophobic (water-repelling) and oleophobic (oil-repelling) — which means a PFAS-treated fabric simultaneously repels sweat stains, water, and body oils. That’s why they’ve been used so widely in performance categories.

The concern is that PFAS don’t stay put. They can migrate off fabrics during wear and washing, and they accumulate in the body and environment over time. Research has associated PFAS exposure with immune system effects, thyroid disruption, and other health outcomes — particularly at sustained exposure levels.

Where PFAS May Appear in Product Listings

PFAS in performance fabrics aren’t always disclosed. Here’s where to look for signals in a product listing:

Explicit mentions:
- “DWR treated” (common in outerwear)
- “Stain-resistant” (common in activewear and casual wear)
- “Quick-dry finish” (sometimes but not always PFAS-related)
- “Scotchgard™” or “Teflon™” branded treatments

Implicit signals:
- A jacket or activewear top described as both “water-resistant” and “stain-resistant” is more likely PFAS-treated
- “Performance coating” without further detail
- Claims of “lasting freshness” or “long-term odor resistance” via chemical treatment (different from natural odor resistance in merino or silver-ion treatments)

Absence of disclosure:
Most performance fabrics are simply listed with their fiber content and care instructions. The absence of a PFAS-free claim doesn’t confirm PFAS presence — but it also doesn’t rule it out. Certification is the gap-closer.

How to Read Claims Carefully

Performance claims vary in what they actually tell you. Here’s how to parse the most common ones:

“Moisture-wicking”
This feature is usually about fiber structure, not chemical treatment. Polyester and nylon wick moisture through capillary action — they pull sweat away from skin and spread it across the fabric surface to evaporate. Most moisture-wicking fabrics aren’t treated with PFAS for that function specifically. However, they may have additional treatments for other functions.

“Quick-dry”
Like moisture-wicking, quick-dry performance is largely structural — synthetic fibers dry faster than cotton because they don’t absorb water. Chemical quick-dry coatings exist but aren’t the norm. Lower concern.

“Water-resistant” in activewear
This one is more significant. Water resistance in a non-outerwear context (a running jacket, a hiking shirt) typically involves a surface treatment. That treatment may or may not be PFAS-based. Ask or look for PFAS-free certification.

“Stain-resistant”
This is the highest-probability signal. Stain resistance in fabric is almost always applied via a chemical finish, and fluorochemical treatments have historically been the most effective approach. When this claim appears, check for a PFAS-free statement before purchasing.

Safer Performance Alternatives

You don’t have to give up performance to avoid PFAS. Here’s what to look for:

Natural high-performance fibers:
- Merino wool: naturally moisture-wicking, odor-resistant, and temperature-regulating without chemical treatments
- Linen blends: breathable and moisture-wicking
- Bamboo viscose: soft and moisture-absorbing (note that bamboo is a marketing claim — check the fiber label, which will say “viscose” if processed)

PFAS-free synthetic options:
- Certified bluesign® polyester or nylon (these fabrics have been produced under a restricted substances program)
- OEKO-TEX® certified activewear
- Brands with explicit PFAS-free commitment (Patagonia’s Capilene® base layers, for example, are made from recycled polyester without PFAS treatment)

FAQ

Does moisture-wicking fabric always contain PFAS?
No. Moisture-wicking is primarily a structural property of synthetic fibers, not a chemical treatment. PFAS are more commonly associated with water repellency and stain resistance. A moisture-wicking polyester running shirt is less likely to contain PFAS than a “stain-resistant” hiking shirt.

Are PFAS used in sports bras and leggings?
Some activewear is treated with stain or water resistance, which could involve PFAS. Base-layer leggings and sports bras without these explicit claims are lower concern. Look for OEKO-TEX® certification for added confidence.

Can I wash PFAS out of performance clothing?
Washing doesn’t remove PFAS from the fabric — it may actually release some PFAS into the wash water. The chemicals are bonded during manufacturing.

What’s the difference between C8 and C6 PFAS in fabric?
C8 (eight-carbon chain) PFAS are the original long-chain compounds with the most research-backed concerns. Most brands have moved to C6 (six-carbon chain) as a “safer” alternative, though C6 PFAS also accumulate in the environment and body. Both are still PFAS; “C6” doesn’t mean PFAS-free.
