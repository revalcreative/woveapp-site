Why Laundry Releases Microplastics

When synthetic fabrics (polyester, nylon, acrylic, fleece) are washed, the mechanical action of the washing machine — water forcing through fibers, clothes tumbling against each other, drum rotation — loosens tiny fiber fragments from the fabric surface. These fragments are typically between 1 and 5 millimeters in length and extremely thin.

Standard washing machine drain filters aren’t designed to catch particles this small. The fibers pass through and enter the wastewater system. Wastewater treatment plants catch a significant percentage — estimates range from 70% to 99% depending on the plant’s technology — but what escapes (even 1% from billions of loads globally) accumulates in the environment.

Research estimates that laundry is one of the largest sources of primary microplastic pollution in the ocean, accounting for hundreds of thousands of tonnes annually.

Washing Habits That Help

Cold water consistently: Hot water causes more fiber degradation per wash. Switching to cold for all synthetic loads is the most impactful single habit change.

Gentle cycles: The delicate or hand-wash cycle reduces mechanical agitation. Less agitation means less fiber release per cycle.

Full loads: A machine that’s too empty has clothes tumbling and rubbing against each other more vigorously. A full (not overloaded) machine cushions fabric-to-fabric contact.

Less detergent: More soap doesn’t clean better — it adds more chemical action and foaming, which increases fiber release. Measure to the recommended amount or slightly below.

Shorter cycles: Less time agitating = less total shedding. Quick-wash settings are sufficient for most activewear if it’s not heavily soiled.

Wash less frequently: Items that can be worn multiple times between washes (merino wool is the prime example) should be. Air garments out between uses rather than defaulting to washing after every wear.

Laundry Tools That May Reduce Shedding

Guppyfriend Washing Bag (most effective consumer tool):
A specially engineered laundry bag that you place synthetic garments inside before putting them in the machine. Its filter structure captures fibers that shed during the wash cycle. Research found it captures a significant portion of shed fibers. Captured fibers are collected in the bag corners and should be disposed in solid waste (not rinsed down the drain).

Cora Ball:
A ball placed in the machine (not a bag — just placed loose in the drum). Captures some microfibers as it tumbles with the clothes. Studies show more variable results than the Guppyfriend bag, but it requires less effort and catches fibers from everything in the load.

PlanetCare Filter / Lint LUV-R (most comprehensive):
These are external filters installed at your washing machine’s drain outlet. They filter the outgoing water, capturing microfibers from everything washed — not just what’s in a bag. Installation requires basic plumbing and a one-time setup. The most comprehensive household solution available.

Washing machine manufacturers’ built-in filters:
Some newer washing machine models include microfiber filters in their design (Samsung, Bosch, and others have introduced these in some markets). Worth checking your machine’s specifications or manufacturer pages.

Better Fabric Choices Over Time

The most impactful long-term intervention isn’t a laundry tool — it’s choosing natural fibers for frequently washed garments. A merino wool t-shirt worn 4 times per wear cycle and then washed gently sheds approximately zero plastic fibers per wash. A polyester t-shirt washed after every wear sheds thousands per wash.

If you’re making any new clothing purchases, prioritize:

  • Natural fiber options (merino, linen, GOTS cotton) for daily-wear items and activewear
  • Certified synthetics (bluesign®, OEKO-TEX®) when performance requires synthetic
  • Items designed to wear multiple times between washes

You don’t need to replace everything at once. Start with your most-washed items — typically activewear — and the impact adds up over time.

FAQ

Does a microfiber filter in the machine catch everything?
No filter catches 100% of microfibers. External drain filters (PlanetCare, Lint LUV-R) capture a high proportion — some studies show over 90% — but there are always some that pass through.

Do I need both a Guppyfriend bag AND a drain filter?
You don’t need both, but using both maximizes capture. A drain filter covers everything; a Guppyfriend bag reduces what sheds in the first place by providing a more protective environment for garments during washing.

Is fabric softener a problem for microplastic shedding?
Yes. Fabric softeners coat fibers and can actually loosen more surface fibers over time. Skip fabric softener for synthetic garments specifically.

What should I do with captured microfibers from the Guppyfriend bag?
Collect them from the bag corners and dispose in your household solid waste bin. Don’t rinse them down the drain — that defeats the purpose.
